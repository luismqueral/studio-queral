i sent a message to the design org the other day during our Claude Code rollout. it was one of those things where you type it fast and hit send because you can feel the room getting weird. people were quiet in a way that usually means they're either confused or scared or both.

the message was basically: you're still a valuable designer. roles will change but craft won't. if you're overwhelmed, you're not alone. and then a list of all the things we're collectively trying to figure out at the same time.

i want to expand on that a little.

</div>

---

<div class="mw6 center tl mb4">

## the list

here's what's actually on a designer's plate right now, all at once:

- new tools — Figma Make, Claude Code, Cursor, whatever shipped this week
- a new interaction paradigm where you talk to your tools instead of clicking through them, and even the people building these things will tell you they don't fully understand the implications
- development workflows — git, deployments, CI/CD, observability — that seasoned engineers still find frustrating on a regular basis
- shifting role boundaries, where it's increasingly unclear where design stops and engineering starts (and whether that boundary was ever real to begin with)
- corporate and industry pressure to "adopt AI" that rolls downhill from leadership to ICs with very little nuance along the way
- the open question of whether and how any of this fits into your actual workflow, or if it's just noise

that's not a normal amount of change. that's a phase transition. and most people are trying to process it while also doing their regular job.

</div>

<div class="mw6 center tl mb4">

## the part nobody says out loud

there's a specific kind of anxiety that comes from watching your tools change faster than your understanding of them. it's not imposter syndrome exactly — it's more like the ground moved and you're still standing but you're not sure where.

and the hard thing is that it's layered. it's not just "I don't know how to use Cursor." it's "I don't know how to use Cursor, and I'm not sure if I should learn Cursor, and I'm not sure if learning Cursor will matter in six months, and also my role might be different by then, and also I still have a sprint to deliver."

that's a lot of uncertainty stacked on top of itself. and people are not going to tell you they're feeling that way in standup.

</div>

---

<div class="mw6 center tl mb4">

## craft doesn't change

here's the thing I keep coming back to: the underlying work of design doesn't go away when the tools change. it never has.

knowing how to frame a problem well. knowing when a solution is solving the wrong thing. reading a room. understanding what a user actually needs versus what they're asking for. sequencing complexity so people can follow along. making hard tradeoffs and standing behind them.

none of that is threatened by a code generation model. none of that gets automated. if anything, it gets more important — because when the tools can produce more output faster, the judgment layer becomes the bottleneck. taste, framing, and critical thinking are doing more load-bearing work than ever.

the designers who were strong before this shift are still strong. the things that made them strong — rigor, curiosity, craft, judgment — are the same things that will make them strong in whatever comes next.

</div>

<div class="mw6 center tl mb4">

## roles change, and that's not new

every few years the design industry has an identity crisis about what designers are supposed to do. are we researchers? are we systems thinkers? are we prototypers? should we code? should we write? should we do strategy?

the answer has always been: it depends, and it shifts, and you adapt.

the current shift is bigger than most, I'll give you that. the boundary between design and engineering is getting genuinely blurry. designers are writing code, sometimes without realizing it. engineers are making design decisions, sometimes without realizing it. the tools are dissolving a border that was always somewhat artificial.

but roles have always been more fluid than job descriptions suggest. the designers who thrive tend to be the ones who hold their role identity loosely — who define themselves by what they're good at rather than by what their title says they should be doing.

</div>

---

<div class="mw6 center tl mb4">

## the permission to not know

i think the most useful thing I can say right now is: you don't have to have this figured out.

nobody does. not the people building the tools. not the people writing the thought pieces. not the people posting confidently on LinkedIn. nobody has a stable mental model of where this is going because the landscape is moving too fast for stable mental models.

what you can do is stay curious without putting pressure on yourself to master everything at once. pick one thing. try it. see if it sticks. if it doesn't, try a different thing next week. that's not falling behind — that's the only reasonable strategy when the terrain is shifting this fast.

and if your honest answer right now is "I just need to focus on my current work and I'll deal with this later" — that's also fine. the tools will still be there. they'll probably be better by then, actually.

</div>

---

<div class="mw6 center tl mb4">

## the real work

the real work right now isn't learning any specific tool. it's developing a relationship with uncertainty that lets you keep going. it's trusting that the skills you've built — the real ones, the design ones, not the "which keyboard shortcut opens the plugin panel" ones — are durable.

tools are always in flux. craft compounds. that's been true for the entire history of this profession and there's no reason to believe it stops being true now.
